  The Settled Fact. Frazell didn't make a case. He pronounced one.
  The claim is offered as already proven, decades old, beyond
  dispute. Dissent is sorted into a cult ("Sowell backers, real
  estate speculators"). The structure compresses three moves into a
  single comment: claim-as-fact, dissent-as-cult, and the dare to
  argue with a man who has already declared the matter closed.

  Dismissive One-Liner archetype. Short, scornful, walks past the
  argument as if the argument is beneath him.
trap: |
  The instinct is to litigate the studies. Card and Krueger versus
  Neumark and Wascher. CBO methodology. Hours of empirical
  back-and-forth. That is the trap.

  Once you are inside the studies, you have accepted his frame.
  The question is settled, in his telling, by counting jobs lost
  in some narrow window of the literature, not by reasoning from
  first principles. The audience cannot follow it. The louder,
  more confident voice wins by default. Frazell came confident. He
  wins by attrition.
frame_shift: |
  Don't litigate the studies. Audit the logic.

  Three questions, asked in order. Does the price of a good affect
  demand for it? Is the wage the price of labor? If you force the
  price of labor above what some workers can produce, will
  employers still hire them? "Yes" to the first two has already
  answered the third. The unemployment effect is not a Sowell
  claim. It is arithmetic.

  Then bring the data, but only as confirmation. The Congressional
  Budget Office projected 1.4 million job losses from a $15 federal
  floor. The same nonpartisan agency that scores every bill
  Congress writes. Logic first. Data as receipt.

  Then name the move publicly. He absorbed a position. He did not
  reason to one. The proof is in his own comment: he asserted the
  conclusion while denying the arithmetic that produces it. A
  person reasoning from first principles cannot make that error. A
  person running a script can.
one_shot: "What you have is a script. What I have is sound logic and data."
